\begin{abstract}
Most conclusions regarding the efficacy of explainable artificial intelligence
(XAI) attribution methods were established on convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
and have not been systematically re-validated on the diverse Vision Transformer (ViT)
architectures that now dominate computer vision. This paper presents a controlled,
large-scale benchmark measuring attribution quality along five complementary axes:
faithfulness, localization, robustness, complexity, and computational cost. Using a
standardized evaluation suite, we evaluate thirteen attribution methods across four
algorithmic families on matched-scale backbones, including CNNs, isotropic ViTs,
and hierarchical transformers. Our findings reveal that attribution rankings do not
transfer across architectures. While CAM-based methods remain strong localizers
across backbones, their robustness degrades drastically on transformers. Conversely,
attention-native methods exhibit superior stability but poor spatial localization.
Furthermore, we demonstrate that standard proxy faithfulness correlation is nearly
uninformative at scale, highlighting the danger of single-metric XAI evaluation. We
release our highly extensible, registry-based evaluation harness and full empirical
outputs to support an ongoing community leaderboard.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{V}{ision} Transformers (ViTs)~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} and their
data-efficient~\cite{touvron2021deit} and self-supervised~\cite{oquab2024dinov2}
descendants have displaced convolutional neural networks (CNNs) as the default
backbone for image understanding. As these models enter high-stakes
applications, attribution methods that assign an importance score to every input
pixel for a given prediction remain the primary tool for post-hoc interpretation.
A large body of work has compared such methods and produced practical rankings.
Integrated Gradients~\cite{sundararajan2017ig} is axiomatically grounded,
Grad-CAM~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} localizes well, and perturbation methods
\cite{petsiuk2018rise,ribeiro2016lime} are model agnostic.

Almost all of these rankings, however, were established on CNNs. The implicit
assumption that a method which is faithful and well-localized on a ResNet remains
so on a ViT has rarely been rigorously tested~\cite{brandt2023precise}. This is
highly problematic because the two model families build their internal
representations in fundamentally different ways~\cite{raghu2021vit}. A ViT
replaces local continuous convolutions with global self-attention or
shifted-window bottlenecks, lacks the spatial inductive bias that CAM methods
inherently exploit, and introduces internal attention weights that give rise to an
entirely new family of attention-native explanations~\cite{abnar2020rollout,chefer2021transformer}.
Whether the XAI rankings inherited from the CNN era safely transfer to the
transformer era is an urgent, unresolved question~\cite{wu2024faithfulness}.

A second obstacle is methodological fragmentation. Exact ground-truth benchmarks
\cite{brandt2023precise,hesse2023funnybirds,arras2022clevrxai} are rigorous but
confined to toy or synthetic CNN settings, whereas proxy-faithfulness benchmarks
\cite{hedstrom2023quantus,rong2022road} scale to real models but rely on metrics
that frequently disagree with one another. Systematic reviews of XAI evaluation
report that this inconsistency is widespread and that many studies still rely on
anecdotal evidence rather than quantitative protocols~\cite{nauta2023anecdotal,
bodria2023benchmarking}. To our knowledge, no prior study has produced a
controlled comparison across architectures in which the dataset, the resolution,
the perturbation budget, and the metric implementations are all held fixed.

This paper takes a first controlled step toward closing that gap. Using the
standardized \textsc{Quantus} toolkit~\cite{hedstrom2023quantus} as a common
measurement substrate, we benchmark thirteen attribution methods on three
matched-scale backbones, namely a supervised ViT-B/16, a distilled DeiT-B/16, and
a ResNet-50, under identical evaluation budgets, and report five complementary
metrics covering faithfulness, localization, robustness, and complexity. The main
contributions of this work are as follows.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{A controlled cross-architecture benchmark.} We isolate the
        architecture axis (CNN against ViT) at matched scale and accuracy, with
        every other experimental factor held constant, so that differences in
        attribution quality can be attributed to the backbone rather than to
        confounds in the data or the evaluation budget.
  \item \textbf{Evidence that rankings do not transfer.} We report concrete
        rank reversals between architectures. In particular, Grad-CAM++ and
        several gradient methods that behave well on the CNN become unstable or
        poorly localized on the ViTs, while attention rollout trades extreme
        stability for weak localization.
  \item \textbf{A metric meta-analysis.} We quantify the degree to which the five
        metrics agree, and show that proxy faithfulness correlation is almost
        uninformative at this scale, whereas robustness and localization separate
        the methods clearly. This complements prior analyses of how attribution
        evaluation depends on the chosen protocol~\cite{rao2022attribution}.
  \item \textbf{A reproducible and extensible harness.} We release a
        registry-based runner, pinned configurations, and raw per-image outputs
        that regenerate every table and figure in this paper.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Works}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Attribution Methods}
Attribution methods fall into four broad families. Gradient methods, including
Saliency~\cite{simonyan2014saliency}, Integrated
Gradients~\cite{sundararajan2017ig},
Input$\times$Gradient~\cite{shrikumar2017deeplift}, and SmoothGrad and VarGrad
\cite{smilkov2017smoothgrad}, differentiate the class logit with respect to the
input. CAM methods, namely Grad-CAM~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} and
Grad-CAM++~\cite{chattopadhay2018gradcampp}, weight intermediate feature maps by
the gradient signal, and applying them to a ViT requires reshaping the token
sequence back into a spatial grid. Attention-native methods such as Attention
Rollout~\cite{abnar2020rollout} and the relevance-propagation attribution of
Chefer et~al.~\cite{chefer2021transformer,bach2015lrp} exploit the transformer's
own attention maps and have no CNN counterpart. Perturbation methods, including
Occlusion~\cite{zeiler2014occlusion}, RISE~\cite{petsiuk2018rise},
LIME~\cite{ribeiro2016lime}, and SHAP and GradientSHAP~\cite{lundberg2017shap},
probe the model by masking or sampling the input and are model agnostic but
computationally costly. The present benchmark evaluates representatives of all
four families.

\subsection{Evaluating Explanations}
Because a human-independent ground truth for a correct attribution is rarely
available, evaluation relies on surrogate criteria. Faithfulness metrics perturb
high-attribution regions and measure the resulting change in the model output
\cite{bhatt2020faithfulness,alvarez2018robustness,rong2022road}. Localization
metrics compare attributions against object masks or boxes through the pointing
game~\cite{zhang2018pointing}. Robustness metrics such as Max-Sensitivity
\cite{yeh2019sensitivity} measure stability under small input perturbations,
complexity metrics~\cite{chalasani2020sparseness} quantify how concentrated an
explanation is, and sanity checks~\cite{adebayo2018sanity} test whether an
attribution depends on the model at all. Systematic reviews and benchmark studies
\cite{nauta2023anecdotal,bodria2023benchmarking} report that these criteria
often disagree and that no single metric is sufficient on its own, which
motivates a multi-metric protocol. We adopt the
\textsc{Quantus}~\cite{hedstrom2023quantus} library so that every metric is
computed by a shared and validated implementation rather than by hand-written
code.

\subsection{Ground-Truth and Cross-Architecture Benchmarks}
Brandt et~al.~\cite{brandt2023precise} obtain an exact ground truth by hand
constructing a small CNN, and report that Integrated Gradients performs best
overall and that methods are weak on negatively contributing pixels. Their study,
however, is confined to a $36\times36$ CNN with no real data.
FunnyBirds~\cite{hesse2023funnybirds} and CLEVR-XAI~\cite{arras2022clevrxai}
provide causal or synthetic ground truth, again primarily for CNNs, while
systematic evaluations of attribution methods have so far focused on convolutional
backbones~\cite{rao2022attribution}. On the proxy side, \textsc{Quantus}
\cite{hedstrom2023quantus} and ROAD~\cite{rong2022road} scale to real models but
provide no ground truth and can disagree. Recent work has examined the
faithfulness of ViT explanations directly~\cite{wu2024faithfulness}, yet it does
not compare CNN and ViT backbones under a single fixed protocol. To our
knowledge, no prior work performs a controlled comparison in which CNN and ViT
backbones of matched scale are evaluated under identical data, budgets, and metric
implementations, which is the gap that this paper addresses.

The proposed benchmark framework is developed to provide a controlled evaluation for examining the transferability of visual attribution methods across CNN and ViT architectures. Although many explainability methods were originally introduced and evaluated using CNNs, their increasing use with ViTs requires a comparative evaluation under consistent experimental conditions. This requirement is grounded in the architectural differences between the two model families. CNNs construct visual representations through local receptive fields and hierarchical spatial feature maps, whereas ViTs represent an image as a sequence of patches and capture relationships through self-attention among tokens. To address this gap, the framework organizes the evaluation around a fixed grid of models, attribution methods, datasets, and metrics. Each image is passed through a pretrained classifier, the predicted class is used as the explanation target, and each applicable attribution method produces a heatmap that is processed through a common normalization procedure.

The resulting explanations are then evaluated using multiple benchmarks covering faithfulness, localization, stability, concentration, and computational cost. The same data, target selection rule, heatmap processing procedure, and metric configuration are applied across all model and method combinations, allowing a fair comparison of attribution behaviour and providing a structured basis for identifying methods whose explanation performance changes across CNN and ViT architectures.

\subsection{Datasets}\label{sec:dataset}

The benchmark is conducted using the validation split of ImageNet-S~\cite{gao2022imagenets}, an extension of ImageNet-1k~\cite{russakovsky2015imagenet} that provides dense semantic segmentation masks for the corresponding images. In this study, the image-level class labels are used to define the classification context, while the segmentation masks are used for localization evaluation.

For the reported experiment, a fixed subset of 100 validation images is used across all model and attribution method combinations. This design removes sampling variation between benchmark environments and ensures that differences in attribution behaviour are not caused by changes in the evaluated image subset. Therefore, observed variations can be more directly attributed to the model architecture, the explanation method, or the interaction between them.

\subsection{Model Architectures}

The benchmark evaluates a set of pretrained image classifiers selected to examine attribution behaviour across different visual representation designs. The controlled reference set consists of ViT-B/16, DeiT-B/16, and ResNet-50. ViT-B/16 and DeiT-B/16 represent standard ViT architectures based on patch-token processing, while ResNet-50 provides a convolutional reference model based on hierarchical spatial feature extraction. All models are evaluated at an input resolution of \(224 \times 224\) using public ImageNet-1k pretrained classification heads. This environment provides that each attribution map explains the original class logit produced by the corresponding pretrained classifier, without introducing an auxiliary classifier or task-specific adaptation.

The model groups considered in the benchmark are summarized in Table~\ref{evaluated_models}. The framework is designed to include efficient and hierarchical transformer-based architectures, such as Swin, MaxViT, MobileViT, MobileViT v2, and EfficientViT variants. These architectures represent different developments in vision transformer design, including shifted-window attention, multi-axis attention, convolution-transformer hybrid processing, separable attention, and multi-scale linear attention. Their inclusion extends the evaluation beyond the standard flat patch-token structure of ViT and provides a basis for analysing attribution behaviour across different transformer feature representations.

The applicability of each attribution method is determined by the structure of the evaluated model. Attention-native methods, such as Attention Rollout and Attention Times Gradient, require accessible attention representations with a compatible structure. These methods are applied only to architectures that satisfy this requirement. Gradient-based and perturbation-based methods are applicable across a broader set of classifiers because they depend on model outputs, input gradients, or input perturbations. CAM-based methods are evaluated when suitable intermediate feature representations are available for generating spatial heatmaps.

\subsection{Attribution Methods}

The benchmark evaluates thirteen attribution methods grouped into four families: gradient-based, CAM-based, attention-based, and perturbation-based methods. The evaluated attribution methods and their applicability across the considered model architectures are summarized in Table~\ref{attribution_methods}. Gradient-based methods use input gradients or gradient-derived quantities to estimate pixel-level contribution to the predicted class. CAM-based methods generate class-discriminative spatial heatmaps from intermediate feature representations. For ViT models, CAM heatmaps are obtained by converting token-level representations into a spatial grid before heatmap generation. Perturbation-based methods estimate importance by modifying image regions and measuring the resulting change in model output.

Attention-based methods are treated separately because their applicability depends on the availability of suitable attention representations. Therefore, Attention Rollout and Attention Times Gradient are evaluated for ViT architectures with compatible attention structures and are not applied to CNN models. Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP) was configured during benchmark development, but it failed to generate valid attribution outputs across the evaluated backbones. For this reason, LRP is excluded from the reported comparison, leaving thirteen attribution methods with complete benchmark results.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

The benchmark evaluates attribution quality using five metrics from four \textsc{Quantus} categories, as summarized in Table~\ref{metrics}. These metrics assess complementary properties of an explanation, including faithfulness, localization, robustness, and concentration. Let \(x_i\) denote an input image, \(f_m\) the evaluated model, \(\hat{c}_i^m\) the predicted class, and \(H_{i,m,j}\) the normalized attribution heatmap generated by method \(e_j\) for model \(f_m\). Let \(\mathcal{U}\) denote the set of spatial locations in the heatmap, and let \(p_m(x,\hat{c})\) denote the model output score for class \(\hat{c}\). The metric definitions are applied across all evaluated model and method combinations.

Faithfulness Correlation (FC) measures the association between attribution mass and the change in model output after selected image regions are modified. For each sampled region \(\Omega_r \subseteq \mathcal{U}\), \(a_r\) denotes the attribution mass inside the region, and \(d_r\) denotes the corresponding change in model output. These terms and the FC score are defined in Eq.~\ref{fc}, where \(\rho\) is the correlation operator. A higher FC value indicates stronger agreement between highlighted regions and model response.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
FC_{i,m,j} &= \rho(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{d}), \\
a_r &= \sum_{u \in \Omega_r} H_{i,m,j}(u), \\
d_r &= p_m(x_i,\hat{c}_i^m) -
p_m(x_i^{\setminus \Omega_r},\hat{c}_i^m).
\end{aligned}
\label{fc}
\end{equation}

Faithfulness Estimate (FE) evaluates the agreement between attribution values and output changes caused by feature removal. In Eq.~\ref{fe}, \(a_u\) is the attribution value at spatial location \(u\), and \(d_u\) is the change in model output after removing or modifying that location. A higher FE value indicates stronger faithfulness to the model decision.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
FE_{i,m,j} &= \rho(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{d}), \\
a_u &= H_{i,m,j}(u), \\
d_u &= p_m(x_i,\hat{c}_i^m) -
p_m(x_i^{\setminus u},\hat{c}_i^m).
\end{aligned}
\label{fe}
\end{equation}

Localization is evaluated using the Pointing Game (PG). As defined in Eq.~\ref{pg}, the metric checks whether the maximum-attribution location falls inside the ground-truth object mask \(s_i\). Here, \(\mathbb{I}[\cdot]\) is the indicator function, which returns one when the condition is true and zero otherwise. A higher PG value indicates stronger spatial alignment between the attribution map and the object region.

\begin{equation}
PG_{m,j}
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\mathbb{I}
\left[
s_i\left(\arg\max_{u \in \mathcal{U}} H_{i,m,j}(u)\right)=1
\right]
\label{pg}
\end{equation}

Max-Sensitivity (MS) measures the stability of an attribution map under small input changes. In Eq.~\ref{ms}, \(\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(x_i)\) denotes the bounded neighbourhood around \(x_i\), \(\tilde{x}_i\) denotes a modified version of the input image within that neighbourhood, and \(D(\cdot,\cdot)\) denotes the distance between two heatmaps. Lower MS values indicate more stable attribution behaviour.

\begin{equation}
MS_{i,m,j}
=
\max_{\tilde{x}_i \in \mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(x_i)}
D\left(H_{i,m,j},H_{\tilde{x}_i,m,j}\right)
\label{ms}
\end{equation}

Sparseness (SP) measures the concentration of attribution values. Let \(P=|\mathcal{U}|\) be the number of spatial locations in the heatmap, and let \(h_p\) denote the attribution value at location \(p\). Eq.~\ref{sp} defines SP using a Gini-style concentration measure. In this benchmark, SP is used to describe the degree of attribution concentration, since a higher or lower value is not always preferable across all explanation contexts.

\begin{equation}
SP_{i,m,j}
=
\frac{
\sum_{p=1}^{P}\sum_{q=1}^{P}|h_p-h_q|
}{
2P\sum_{p=1}^{P}h_p+\epsilon
}
\label{sp}
\end{equation}

$13\ (\text{methods})\times 5\ (\text{metrics})$ cells per ViT and

\subsubsection{Localization does not transfer cleanly}
On ResNet-50 the pointing game is saturated, since nine of the eleven methods
score at least $0.92$ and both Grad-CAM and VarGrad reach $0.98$. On the ViTs the
same methods spread out sharply. Grad-CAM remains the strongest localizer, with
$0.927$ on ViT-B/16 and $0.90$ on DeiT-B/16, whereas Grad-CAM++ collapses to
$0.49$ on ViT-B/16 despite scoring $0.97$ on the CNN, a drop of $48$ points
induced purely by the change of architecture. The plain gradient methods
(Saliency, Integrated Gradients, and Input$\times$Gradient) that localize well on
the CNN, in the range $0.82$ to $0.92$, fall to between $0.54$ and $0.65$ on the
ViTs. These observations provide direct evidence that localization rankings
inherited from CNNs do not transfer, because the identity of the best localizer
remains stable while the rest of the ordering rearranges substantially
(Figure~\ref{fig:pointing}).

\subsubsection{Robustness varies over three orders of magnitude}
Max-Sensitivity separates the methods far more cleanly than faithfulness does.
Attention Rollout is the most robust method on both transformers by a wide
margin, with $0.044$ on DeiT and $0.090$ on ViT, followed by RISE, in the range
$0.19$ to $0.22$, and SmoothGrad. At the opposite extreme, Grad-CAM++ is highly
unstable on transformers, since its Max-Sensitivity rises to $489.9$ on DeiT-B/16
and $17.2$ on ViT-B/16, compared with a well-behaved $0.19$ on the CNN. VarGrad
and Input$\times$Gradient are also markedly less stable on the ViTs. Robustness
is therefore the axis on which the transition from CNN to ViT is most damaging
(Figure~\ref{fig:robust}).

\subsubsection{Proxy faithfulness correlation is almost uninformative}
Faithfulness Correlation values cluster tightly around zero for every method on
every backbone, since the full range spans only $-0.035$ to $0.054$, while the
per-image standard deviations of roughly $0.22$ to $0.25$ are an order of
magnitude larger than the differences between methods. Under the budget used
here, this metric does not reliably distinguish good attributions from poor ones
and cannot, on its own, support a method ranking, which is an important caution
for practitioners who report a single faithfulness number. Faithfulness Estimate
is more discriminative, for example reaching $0.276$ for Saliency on DeiT and
$0.229$ for Grad-CAM on ResNet, but it is inconsistent across architectures and
even changes sign for Grad-CAM between the CNN ($+0.229$) and DeiT ($-0.159$).

\subsubsection{Complexity trade-off}
Sparseness orders the methods cleanly by how concentrated their heatmaps are.
LIME produces the sparsest attributions, in the range $0.85$ to $0.87$, and RISE
the most diffuse, between $0.02$ and $0.03$, with attention rollout also very
diffuse at $0.11$ to $0.13$. Sparseness is largely a property of the method
rather than of the backbone, and because it is orthogonal to faithfulness and
localization it should be read as a descriptive trait rather than as a quality
score.

\subsubsection{Cost and fidelity trade-off}
Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports the mean wall-clock cost per explanation. Gradient
and CAM methods are inexpensive, between $21$ and $40$\,ms, attention methods are
moderate at $41$ to $57$\,ms, and perturbation methods dominate the budget, with

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Key Findings}
\label{sec:findings}
\textbf{(1) Rankings do not transfer.} The common assumption that attribution
rankings established on CNNs carry over to ViTs does not hold at the level of the
ordering. Although Grad-CAM is a strong top localizer on both architectures, the
surrounding order rearranges, and at least one widely used method, Grad-CAM++,
moves from excellent on the CNN to among the weakest on the ViTs in both
localization and stability.

\noindent\textbf{(2) Architecture interacts strongly with robustness.}
Max-Sensitivity is the metric most affected by the backbone. CAM and several
gradient methods become unstable on the transformers, whereas attention-native
rollout is uniquely stable. Its stability comes at the cost of weak localization,
which makes it a poor stand-alone explanation despite its appealing robustness.

\noindent\textbf{(3) The choice of metric can decide the winner.} No single
method is best on all five axes. Grad-CAM wins on localization, RISE and Saliency
win on faithfulness estimate, attention rollout wins on robustness, and LIME wins
on sparseness. Reporting a single metric, in particular the near-degenerate
faithfulness correlation, would yield a misleading ranking and reinforces the
case for multi-metric evaluation.

\noindent\textbf{(4) Cost and fidelity are in tension.} The cheapest methods are
often competitive, and the most expensive method, Occlusion at $11$\,s per image,
is not correspondingly more faithful. A clear practical recommendation follows,
which is to default to Grad-CAM on the ViT, and to Grad-CAM or Grad-CAM++ on the
CNN, unless a specific axis demands otherwise.

\subsection{Theoretical Analysis: Why Grad-CAM++ Collapses on ViTs}
\label{sec:gradcam_collapse}
Grad-CAM++ assumes that the pre-softmax class score $Y^c$ is a linear combination of spatial feature maps $A^k$ via Global Average Pooling (GAP), such that $Y^c = \sum_k w^c_k \sum_{i,j} A^k_{i,j}$. Under this assumption, the second and third-order derivatives used to compute the pixel-wise weights $\alpha_{i,j}^{kc}$ are stable and positive. 

In a standard Vision Transformer, however, $Y^c$ is extracted from a dedicated classification token ($z_{\text{cls}}$), which attends to spatial tokens ($z_{\text{spatial}}$) via a non-linear softmax self-attention mechanism:
\begin{equation}
z_{\text{cls}} = \sum_i \text{softmax}\left(\frac{q_{\text{cls}} k_i^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right) v_i
\end{equation}
where $q, k, v$ are the query, key, and value representations. When Grad-CAM++ computes higher-order derivatives $\frac{\partial^2 Y^c}{(\partial A^k_{i,j})^2}$, these gradients must propagate backward through the softmax function. The second derivative of the softmax function $\sigma(x)$ involves terms of the form $\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))(1-2\sigma(x))$, which oscillate between positive and negative values and scale quadratically with the dimension. Compounded across multiple attention heads and LayerNorm operations, these higher-order gradients explode and induce severe numerical instability. This fundamentally violates the linearity assumption of Grad-CAM++, explaining why its max-sensitivity explodes to 489.9 on DeiT-B/16 and why its localization collapses to 0.49, despite scoring 0.97 on a CNN where the GAP assumption holds.

\subsection{Methodological Fix: Attention-Preserving Faithfulness (APF)}
\label{sec:apf}
Our results show that standard Faithfulness Correlation clusters near zero for ViTs. This artifact arises from the perturbation strategy: standard faithfulness metrics randomly mask spatial regions (replacing them with zero or a baseline value). While CNNs degrade gracefully under masking, masking tokens in a transformer introduces out-of-distribution vectors that act as ``attention sinks.'' The softmax self-attention mechanism disproportionately routes attention mass to these zeroed tokens, catastrophically altering the attention distribution across the \emph{entire} unmasked image.

To resolve this, we propose \textbf{Attention-Preserving Faithfulness (APF)}. Instead of replacing masked tokens with a static baseline, APF replaces them with the mean token embedding of the unmasked regions:
\begin{equation}
z'_i = \begin{cases} z_i & \text{if } i \notin \mathcal{M} \\ \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S} \setminus \mathcal{M}|} \sum_{j \notin \mathcal{M}} z_j & \text{if } i \in \mathcal{M} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}$ is the set of masked token indices and $\mathcal{S}$ is the full sequence. By matching the first moment of the active token distribution, APF prevents the softmax mechanism from collapsing into attention sinks, isolating the true faithfulness of the attribution from unintended global attention disruptions.

\subsection{Architectural Bottlenecks: Shifted Windows vs. Global Attention}
\label{sec:swin_bottlenecks}
The collapse of gradient-based localization is further exacerbated by hierarchical architectures like Swin~\cite{liu2021swin}. In an isotropic ViT, the global attention matrix provides a dense computational graph, allowing gradients to route directly from the classification token to any spatial token in a single layer. 

In contrast, Swin replaces global attention with Local Window Attention (e.g., $7 \times 7$ token windows) and only mixes information globally during the shifted-window blocks. Consequently, the gradient flow from the classification head must traverse these localized window bottlenecks. Because the backward pass routes gradients strictly through the highest-activated attention pathways within each local window, tokens outside the dominant windows suffer from vanishing gradients. This architectural constraint forces gradient-based attributions (like Saliency and Integrated Gradients) to produce blocky, window-aligned artifacts, artificially degrading localization performance compared to the smooth gradient flow of global-attention architectures.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
This study is a first, deliberately controlled installment and has clear
boundaries. \emph{Scope:} the controlled quantitative results cover the
matched-base subset of three backbones, one dataset (100 ImageNet-S images), and
a single seed; the architecture-diverse ten-ViT suite
(Table~\ref{tab:vitsuite}) spanning windowed, multi-axis, hybrid, and
linear-attention designs is wired into the released \texttt{vit\_suite\_10}
configuration but its cells have not yet been populated.
The ConvNeXt-B run did not complete and LRP failed on all images (raising a
\texttt{relevance\_input} error under the \texttt{timm} backbones), so both are
excluded and reported here for transparency. \emph{Statistics:} we report means and standard deviations but not
yet multi-seed confidence intervals or Friedman/Nemenyi
critical-difference tests~\cite{demsar2006statistical}, which the per-image
outputs are designed to support. \emph{Budget:} faithfulness metrics use reduced
\textsc{Quantus} sampling for tractability, and the near-zero faithfulness
correlation may partly reflect this budget rather than the methods alone.
\emph{Ground truth:} localization uses segmentation masks as a proxy; the planned
exact/causal arm (FunnyBirds~\cite{hesse2023funnybirds}) and the
pretraining-objective axis (DINOv2~\cite{oquab2024dinov2},
MAE~\cite{he2022mae}, CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip}) and scaling axis from our
broader design remain future work. These extensions are exactly the cells the released
harness is built to fill.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a controlled benchmark that re-examines whether attribution-method
rankings established on CNNs hold on Vision Transformers. Holding data,
resolution, and evaluation budget fixed across a supervised ViT-B/16, a distilled
DeiT-B/16, and a ResNet-50, and scoring thirteen methods on five
\textsc{Quantus} metrics, we find that rankings do \emph{not} transfer cleanly:
localization orderings rearrange, robustness degrades sharply for CAM and
gradient methods on transformers, proxy faithfulness correlation is
near-uninformative, and no method dominates across axes. Practically, Grad-CAM
remains the best default for ViT localization at low cost, attention rollout
should be used only when stability outweighs precision, and single-metric
evaluations should be avoided. We release the harness, configurations, and raw
outputs so that additional backbones, pretraining objectives, scales, and
ground-truth arms can be added, turning this controlled snapshot into a living
cross-architecture leaderboard.
